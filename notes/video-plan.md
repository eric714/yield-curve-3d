# Video plan — yieldcurve3d.com

## First: what I meant by "beat"

A **beat** is one movement in the script — a single thing you say plus the
single thing that happens on screen while you say it. A 6-minute video is
roughly 10–14 beats. Thinking in beats stops you drifting, because each one has
to justify itself: if a beat has no on-screen change, it is talking, and
talking is what makes videos run long.

Below, every beat is a bullet: **what you say** in plain text, and *what you do*
in italics.

# Part 1 — The height of the surface, properly understood

You flagged this and you were right to. Here is the honest version. I had this
wrong earlier and corrected the site; do not use any older description.

The chart draws a surface. The **height** of that surface is a number you get
to choose, and the three choices answer three different questions.

### Mode 1 — Yield

The rate itself. What the government paid to borrow, that day, for that length
of time. This is the view the whole thing was built for and where you should
spend most of your time.

### Mode 2 — Yield minus Fed funds

Every point is measured against **the rate the Fed was charging that same day**.
So the flat zero plane *is* the Fed, and the surface floats above or below it.

Why that is interesting: the Fed sets exactly one interest rate, overnight.
Every other rate on the chart is set by the market. This mode isolates the gap
between the two — how far the market has moved away from where the Fed
currently is.

**The signal: a surface below zero means the market is betting on cuts.** And it
has been right.

| Before | 2-year below the policy rate | Worst |
|---|---|---|
| the 2001 recession | 179 of 272 trading days | −1.63pp |
| the 2008 cuts | 263 of 271 trading days | −1.38pp |
| the 2024 cuts | 325 of 334 trading days | −1.81pp |

The single cleanest example: on **10 September 2007** the two-year sat 1.38
points *below* what the Fed was charging. Eight days later the Fed cut.

**When it is useless:** when the Fed is parked. Through 2009–2014 the policy
rate was a constant 0.125 per cent, so subtracting it changes nothing — that
stretch spans 4.85 points in both modes, the same number to two decimals. Do
not use this mode to talk about QE. It genuinely does not show it.

### Mode 3 — Yield minus 3-month

Every point measured against **that same day's 3-month bill**. This is the slope
of the curve, and anything below the zero plane is an inversion.

The difference from Mode 2 is the baseline, and it matters:

- **Mode 2's baseline is a decision.** The Fed chose that number in a meeting.
- **Mode 3's baseline is a price.** The market set it.

So Mode 2 asks *"what does the market think of the Fed?"* and Mode 3 asks
*"what shape is the curve?"* Different questions, and worth saying out loud in
any video where you switch modes.

## 1. What a yield curve actually is — 6 min
**One idea:** the government borrows for different lengths of time and pays a
different rate for each; that set of rates on one day is the curve.
`#from=2021-08-21&to=2026-08-21&m=level&w=WALCL&v=front&t=dark&s=qe,rec,ev,ff,lines`

- Open cold on the rotating surface. **"This is what it has cost the United
  States government to borrow money, every trading day for the last five
  years."** *No intro card, no greeting.*
- The government borrows by selling bonds. Someone lends it money; it pays
  interest and returns the principal later. *Panel hidden, full-bleed chart.*
- It borrows for many different lengths at once — one month, two years, thirty
  years. Each length has its own interest rate. *Point along the near edge.*
- **MONEY SHOT (aim for 0:60–0:90).** *Switch to the end-on view.* **"Look at
  it end-on and it becomes one line. That line is a yield curve — every rate
  the government paid, on one single day."**
- Left edge one month, right edge thirty years, height is the rate. *Trace it
  with the cursor.*
- Why it usually rises to the right: lending for longer is riskier, so lenders
  want more. *Hover a day; read the readout aloud.*
- *Pin a day.* Read four numbers off the panel — 3-month, 2-year, 10-year,
  30-year. This is real published data, not a model.
- *Rotate slowly back to the default view.* Every ridge behind it is another
  day. Thirty-six years of them.
- Close on the question the series answers: **"The shape of that line changes,
  and the changes tell you things. That is what the rest of these videos are
  about."**

## 2. Steep, flat, inverted — and why anyone cares — 6 min
**One idea:** the shape carries information the level does not.
`#from=2021-09-01&to=2023-12-29&m=level&w=WALCL&v=front&t=dark&s=qe,rec,ev,ff,lines`

- **"Normally it costs more to borrow for ten years than for two. Sometimes it
  costs less. That is worth understanding."**
- 20-second recap for anyone arriving cold. *End-on view.*
- Normal, upward-sloping. *Jump to the "Cuts and steepening" preset.*
- Flat: the market expects nothing much. *Greenspan's conundrum preset.*
- **MONEY SHOT.** *Back to this range.* **"Now watch."** The surface tips
  backwards. Two-year money costs more than ten-year.
- Quote it: on 3 July 2023 the two-year was 4.94 per cent and the ten-year
  3.86. *Pin that day.*
- Why it happens: the market expects the Fed to cut, which means it expects
  weakness ahead.
- **The honest bit, and say it exactly this way:** every recession in this
  record was preceded by an inversion. But the long inversion from 2022 to
  2024 was not followed by one. It is a warning light, not a prophecy.
- *Switch to Mode 3.* Everything below the plane is inverted. **"You can see
  how much of the last two years was."**

## 3. 1994: the year the bond market broke — 5 min
**One idea:** six surprise hikes lifted the entire surface at once.
`#from=1993-10-01&to=1995-06-30&m=level&w=WALCL&v=default&t=dark&s=qe,rec,ev,ff,lines`

- **"In one year the cost of borrowing rose for every single maturity. All of
  them. At once."**
- Set the scene: 1993, rates at 3 per cent, everyone comfortable.
- *Scrub the slider slowly forward through 1994.* **MONEY SHOT: the entire
  sheet rising.**
- The numbers: the smallest move across the whole curve was +1.61 points, the
  largest +2.88. *Pin January 1994, then November.*
- Why it hurt: bond prices fall when yields rise, and nobody was positioned
  for it.
- *Orange stair-step on the front edge.* Six hikes, mostly unannounced. The Fed
  did not publish decisions the way it does now.
- What changed afterwards: this is a large part of why the Fed now telegraphs
  everything.

## 4. The dot-com bust — 6 min
**One idea:** the front end fell off a cliff while the long end barely moved.
`#from=2000-01-03&to=2004-06-30&m=level&w=WALCL&v=default&t=dark&s=qe,rec,ev,ff,lines`

- Open on the inverted 2000 curve. **"This is what the bond market looked like
  at the top of the dot-com bubble."**
- May 2000: 3-month 6.20 per cent, 30-year 6.12. Inverted. *Pin it.*
- *Scrub through 2001.* **MONEY SHOT: the near edge collapsing while the far
  edge holds.**
- Eleven cuts in one year. By December 2001 the 3-month was 1.74 and the
  30-year still 5.48.
- *Turn on the recession band.* It arrives in March 2001, after the inversion.
- Long rates stayed high because long rates are about the next decade, not the
  next meeting. **That is the lesson of this one.**

## 5. Greenspan's conundrum — 6 min
**One idea:** the Fed hiked seventeen times and the long end refused to follow.
`#from=2004-06-01&to=2006-08-31&m=level&w=WALCL&v=side&t=dark&s=qe,rec,ev,ff,lines`

- **"The Fed raised rates seventeen times in a row. The ten-year barely
  moved. Greenspan called it a conundrum, and he was not being modest."**
- Two numbers, and they are the whole video: **Fed funds +4.25 points.
  Ten-year +0.51 points.** *Put them on screen.*
- *Side view.* **MONEY SHOT: the front edge climbing under a far edge that
  stays flat.**
- *Pin June 2004, then June 2006.* 3-month 1.17 → 5.01. Ten-year 4.71 → 5.22.
- The result is a flat curve — the surface almost level from front to back.
- Why it mattered: cheap long-term money kept the housing market going while
  the Fed was trying to cool it.
- Close on the sequel: this flatness tipped into inversion in 2006, and you
  know what happened next. *Sets up video 6.*

## 6. 2008: a crisis in three dimensions — 7 min
**One idea:** inversion, then collapse to zero, as one continuous shape.
`#from=2007-06-01&to=2009-12-31&m=level&w=WALCL&v=default&t=dark&s=qe,rec,ev,ff,lines`

- Open on the inverted 2007 curve, no preamble.
- Recap in fifteen seconds. *End-on, then back.*
- Mid-2007: inverted, and the market already uneasy.
- *Scrub to August 2007.* The event marker. One-month bills fall from 5.04 to
  3.13 in six sessions as the commercial paper market shuts. **First crack.**
- *Scrub to September 2008.* Two days after Lehman the 3-month yields 0.08 per
  cent. **"Lenders were accepting almost nothing to hold government paper for
  three months. That is what panic looks like as a number."**
- **MONEY SHOT: scrub slowly through 2008.** The whole front half falls away
  into deep blue.
- December 2008: the Fed cuts to a range of zero to a quarter per cent for the
  first time in its history. *Front-edge ribbon flattens onto the floor.*
- *Turn on the recession shading.* December 2007 to June 2009.
- Close on where it leaves the curve: front pinned at zero, long end still up.
  The steepest shape in the record. *Sets up video 8.*

## 7. The bond market forecasts the Fed — 7 min ⭐
**One idea:** the market prices cuts months before the Fed makes them, and you
can see it as a shape.
`#from=2007-06-01&to=2009-12-31&m=vsFunds&w=WALCL&v=default&t=dark&s=qe,rec,ev,ff,lines`

- **"In September 2007 the bond market knew the Fed was about to cut. The Fed
  had not said so. Here is what that looks like."**
- Explain the mode carefully — this is the video that teaches it. The Fed sets
  one rate, overnight. Everything else on the chart is set by the market.
- *Switch the height dropdown on camera.* **MONEY SHOT.** The flat plane is now
  the Fed itself. The surface floats above or below it.
- Above the plane: the market wants more than the Fed is paying. Below the
  plane: the market is betting the Fed will cut.
- *Pin 10 September 2007.* Fed charging 5.25 per cent. Two-year at 3.87. **The
  market sat 1.38 points below the Fed.**
- **Eight days later the Fed cut to 4.75.** *Pin 18 September.*
- It was not a fluke: the two-year was below the policy rate on 263 of the 271
  trading days before those cuts.
- And it repeats. 179 of 272 days before the 2001 recession. 325 of 334 before
  the 2024 cuts.
- **Say the limitation out loud:** this mode tells you almost nothing when the
  Fed is parked at zero, because subtracting a constant changes nothing. It is
  a tool for periods when policy is moving.

## 8. QE, told honestly — 7 min ⭐
**One idea:** what the Fed actually did, and what the curve did alongside it —
without claiming the chart proves one caused the other.
`#from=2008-09-01&to=2015-12-31&m=level&w=WALCL&v=default&t=dark&s=qe,rec,ev,ff,lines`

- **"Between 2008 and 2014 the Federal Reserve created about three and a half
  trillion dollars and bought bonds with it. Here is what the curve did while
  that happened."**
- What QE is, plainly: the Fed buys long-dated bonds, hoping to push their
  yields down when it cannot cut the overnight rate any further.
- *Point at the back wall.* The balance sheet. **MONEY SHOT: scrub forward and
  watch it stair-step up behind the surface.**
- The numbers: $0.93 trillion the week Lehman failed. $2.31tn by the end of
  QE1. $4.49tn by the end of QE3. *Pin each.*
- *Floor bands.* QE1, QE2, Operation Twist, QE3, and the taper.
- **The honest part, and do not skip it:** the curve did not simply fall
  through this. The ten-year was 2.37 per cent in December 2008 and 3.84 by
  the end of QE1 — it went *up*. Because those years were also a recovery.
- **"You cannot read causation off this chart, and anyone who tells you
  otherwise is selling something. What you can see is scale, and timing, and
  that the long end stayed remarkably low for remarkably long."**
- Operation Twist as the interesting case: the balance sheet stays flat while
  the long end still falls. *Wall visibly level, surface still moving.*

## 9. The taper tantrum — 5 min
**One idea:** the long end jumped on no policy change at all.
`#from=2012-06-01&to=2015-01-31&m=level&w=WALCL&v=side&t=dark&s=qe,rec,ev,ff,lines`

- **"In May 2013 the Fed changed nothing. Bond yields jumped anyway."**
- The setup: QE3 running, everyone comfortable.

## 12. The week one-month bills paid more than two-month — 6 min ⭐

- **"For a few weeks in 2023, lending to the US government for one month paid

# Part 3 — Production

### Staying inside 5–7 minutes
1. **Write the one idea as a single sentence first.** If it needs an "and", it
   is two videos.
2. **Money shot by 90 seconds.** It is the hook, not the finale.
3. **No greeting.** Open on the chart and a sentence that makes someone want
   the answer.
4. **Every beat needs an on-screen change.** If nothing moves, cut it.
5. **Hard stop at seven.** Leftovers are the next video, and you need topics.

### Mechanics
- **Paste the link, then record.** Never set up controls on camera.
- **Double-click empty chart space** to hide the side panel for full-bleed
  sections, and again to bring it back when you are discussing a control.
- **Pin days by clicking** so the readout stays put while you talk. Escape
  clears it.
- **The `?` button replays the walkthrough** if you want a section for free.
- Record in **dark mode**; export **light mode** stills for thumbnails, because
  they stand out in a feed of dark finance charts.

### Thumbnails
The camera button exports at 2× with the title, date range, sources and
watermark already on it. Frame the money shot, export, use it. The site gets
credited even when someone only sees the preview.

### A note on numbers
Every figure in these scripts was checked against the data. If you want to add
one I have not listed, ask me to verify it first — the site carries an accuracy
disclaimer and the videos should hold to the same standard.
